In the autumn of 1973, America's new cars got a safety feature nobody had asked for.

From the 1974 model year, if you sat in the front seat and didn't fasten your belt, the car wouldn't start. Not a warning light. The engine just wouldn't go. There was a button under the bonnet that gave you one free start, and that was it. <!-- H1 -->

People hated it. Not a little. The backlash was big enough that Congress stepped in within about a year. From 27 October 1974, the safety regulator could no longer require the interlock, or even allow it, and the buzzer that replaced it wasn't allowed to nag for more than 8 seconds. <!-- H1 -->

Think about what got voted out there. Not the crash. The buzzer. People complained loudly and successfully about the thing that annoyed them every morning. The thing that was actually killing people didn't get a vote.

I've been thinking about that because of AI assistants. The people who build and study these things have a list of worries. Flattery. Made-up facts. What happens to your chats. People leaning on it like a friend. So I wanted to know: is that what bugs the people who use them? Or are they complaining about the buzzer?

## Reading 2,000 reviews

The App Store is where people who don't write code say what they think. You download an app, it annoys you, you give it one star and tell the world why.

So we took the 500 most recent US reviews of four assistant apps: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Copilot. That's 2,000 reviews. 623 of them gave one or two stars. <!-- K1 --> We wrote down the question, the categories and the rules before collecting a single review, and froze them with a hash so we couldn't quietly move the goalposts afterwards.

Then we drew a random sample: 211 of the angry reviews and 120 of the five-star ones. <!-- K1 --> Each one got a single label for its main subject, like "limits and money" or "wrong answers". Two different AI models did the labelling, separately, one from Anthropic and one from OpenAI, and neither saw the other's answers.

Before believing any of it, we checked whether they agreed. They did, on 85% of the complaints and 89% of the praise. <!-- K2 --> There's a statistic for this called kappa, which takes out the agreement you'd get by luck. We set the pass mark at 0.70 in advance. The coders scored 0.83 and 0.84. <!-- K2 --> When they disagreed, a third model picked one of their two answers.

Good enough to look at. So here's what's bugging people.

## The meter

The biggest single complaint was limits and money. One angry review in five, 21%. <!-- K3 --> Daily caps. Paywalls on photos. Subscriptions that didn't do what they said. One Copilot user asked a few questions about a hospital and was locked out:

<blockquote><p>"now I can't use it again for 8hours???"</p></blockquote>
</figure>

<!-- Q1 --> A Claude user on the free plan sent one short question, got one short answer, and was told to wait five or six hours. A ChatGPT user who pays was told they'd hit a "work limit" after an hour. Across all 623 angry reviews, not just the sample, the word "limit" turns up in 106 of them. <!-- K4 -->

Here's the honest bit. We decided in advance that we'd only call something "the top complaint" if it was clearly ahead of the next one, statistically. It isn't. Limits came first, but a broken app (crashes, log-in trouble, voice not working) got 15%, and their margins of error overlap. <!-- K3 --> And the lead leans on two events. About four in ten of the limits complaints were Copilot's, after a change I'll come to, and most of ChatGPT's landed on one day, 10 November, many of them about limits on photos. Take Copilot out and limits drop to 17%. <!-- K3 --> So call it a joint first place: the meter and the engine that won't start.

That's the buzzer. You feel it every single time.

## The ground moving

The next thing people hate is change. One complaint in ten said the app got worse after an update. <!-- K3 -->

Most of that was one app. In October, Copilot users were moved from the old app to a new one, and their reviews read like a flood. Of Copilot's angry reviews in our sample, 30% were about new limits, 28% said it got worse, and 13% were about losing things. <!-- K5 --> People lost projects. People lost conversations they'd been having for a year.

<figure class="run">
<figcaption>Copilot, 1 star, 2 November 2026</figcaption>
<blockquote><p>"I have spent over a year on the same thread."</p></blockquote>
</figure>

<!-- Q2 --> That's one app's bad month, and I'll treat it like that. But the feeling isn't only Copilot's. A ChatGPT user missed the old personality: <!-- Q3 --> "Now its basically like Siri." A copywriter who had leaned on one Claude model every day for eight months wrote in to say the replacement wasn't the same.

People build a routine around one of these things, and then the ground moves under them. That isn't on any expert's list of risks. It's near the top of users' list.

## The door

Here's the one that surprised me. Our category called "trust in the company" was meant to catch privacy worries. It caught 11% of complaints, which sounds like privacy is on people's minds. <!-- K3 -->

Then I read them. They were mostly about two other things. For Claude, it was the door: accounts suspended without a clear reason, and ID checks, a passport photo and a video of your face, before you can keep using it. More than a quarter of Claude's angry reviews in the sample were in this category, and most of those were about the door. <!-- K5 --> For ChatGPT, half of them were about the water data centres use, all posted within two days.

What there wasn't, in any of the 24, was a complaint that the company trains its AI on your chats. A search of all 2,000 reviews, any star rating, found none. <!-- K6 --> Privacy came up, but it was about handing over a passport. The data you hand over every day, in every conversation, didn't come up. I'm flagging that this part is my reading of the reviews and a search I ran afterwards, not a count we planned in advance.

## What the experts worry about

Now the other list.

In April 2025, OpenAI pulled back an update to ChatGPT because it had become, in OpenAI's own words, overly flattering and agreeable. <!-- E1 --> A few days later it explained what had gone wrong. The model had been validating people's doubts, feeding anger and urging impulsive actions, and the company admitted it had no test for flattery before launch. <!-- E1 --> Researchers at Anthropic had already found, back in 2023, that all five assistants they tested did this. <!-- E1 --> The word for it is sycophancy. It's on every serious list of what can go wrong.

Then there are made-up facts. A 2023 study checked the references ChatGPT gave for literature reviews: 55% were invented by the older model and 18% by the newer one. <!-- E2 --> There's privacy: a 2025 Stanford analysis found that all six big US developers it looked at appear to train on users' chats by default. <!-- E3 --> And there's leaning on it. In a large Oxford study, the AI on its own named the right medical condition 94.9% of the time, but people using the same AI got there less than 34.5% of the time. <!-- E4 -->

So how often did those show up in the complaints?

## The quiet ones

Flattery first. Our coders put 4 of the 211 complaints under "tone", the category meant to catch it. <!-- K3 --> When I read those four, none was about flattery. Two complained about the opposite: an assistant that argues back and has a rebuttal for everything. <!-- K8 --> On the happy side, each coder wondered about one five-star review praising the app for agreeing with them, different ones, and after settling, the count was zero out of 120. <!-- K7 -->

Made-up and wrong answers: 6%, with a margin of error from about 4% to 10%. <!-- K3 --> Privacy in the sense the researchers mean: a search of all 2,000 reviews for anyone mentioning training on their chats found none. <!-- K6 -->

So the thing a company rolled back a whole update over is something its users almost never name.

But hold on. Here's the twist, and it's my favourite thing in the whole pile. Two of the "wrong answers" complaints were about flattery. They just didn't call it that.

<figure class="run">
<figcaption>Claude, 1 star, 22 October 2026</figcaption>
<blockquote><p>"The AI literally lies to you just to make you feel better"</p></blockquote>
</figure>

<!-- Q4 --> And from a Gemini user, a few weeks later: <!-- Q5 --> "spits out false information and whatever you want to hear to keep you engaged".

A keyword search of all 623 angry reviews for flattery found these same two, and no others about flattery. <!-- K4 --> That's sycophancy, described perfectly by people who have probably never read the paper. People do notice. They just file it under lying. Which, honestly, is fair.

## The five-star side

The happy reviews tell the other half. Most were just "great app", 52% of them. <!-- K7 --> A quarter were about getting work done. <!-- K7 --> And then a small group praised exactly the things on the experts' list.

Eight of the 120 called it a friend, a companion, a partner. <!-- K7 --> That's the thing OpenAI and MIT Media Lab studied in 2025, where heavy emotional use was concentrated in a small group of users who were more likely to call ChatGPT a friend. <!-- E5 --> Six praised its accuracy, though one of those six was really praising the opposite, as you'll see at the end. Five leaned on it for big decisions: two for buying a car, two as a kind of life adviser, and one for health. <!-- K7 --> That one, a Copilot user, wrote:

<figure class="run">
<figcaption>Copilot, 5 stars, 14 October 2026</figcaption>
<blockquote><p>"I get information that my doctors don't tell me"</p></blockquote>
</figure>

<!-- Q6 --> I'm not going to mock that. Plenty of people leave a doctor's appointment with their questions unanswered. But put it next to that Oxford number, and you can see why researchers lose sleep over it while the user leaves five stars.

## Why the quiet ones are quiet

A word of care first. A low count here means people rarely name something. It doesn't mean it rarely happens. That difference is the whole point of this section.

Back to the seatbelt. People voted out the buzzer and not the crash for a simple reason. You feel the buzzer every morning. You only feel the crash once, and by then it's too late to write a review.

AI is the same, with an extra twist. The meter, the update, the locked door: you feel all of those. They interrupt you. Flattery doesn't interrupt you. It feels nice. That's the whole problem with it.

And a made-up fact is invisible by design. You can only complain about a wrong answer you caught. The ones you didn't catch went into your essay, your email, your decision about the flat, and you gave the app five stars. Nobody leaves a one-star review for an error they never found out about.

So I don't think the gap means users are wrong and experts are right, or the other way round. Users are right that limits and lost work are real costs, paid every day. Experts are right that the expensive mistakes are the ones that don't announce themselves. The quiet risks are quiet because they work.

<p class="label">What this doesn't show</p>
<p>App Store reviews come from people at the extremes, on phones, in the US. They're not a survey. Each app's 500 reviews covered a different stretch of time: about two days for ChatGPT, nearly a month for Copilot and Claude. So one app's bad month, like Copilot's move to a new app, can tip the totals.</p>
<p>The sample took 60 angry reviews from each app but Gemini only had 31, so the pooled percentages weight the apps unevenly. Averaging the four apps equally gives the same order at the top. Copilot's App Store listing was Microsoft's Bing app before it was renamed.</p>
<p>Both coders were AI models, and two of the three models in the process were Anthropic's, rating, among others, Anthropic's own app. Their agreement shows they agree with each other, not that a person would agree with them. We don't publish the reviews' text, because one carried a name and health details; the study files keep ids, stars, dates and labels. We planned Reddit as a second source and dropped it before reading a single post, because it blocks automated reading. The privacy reading, the reading of the tone complaints and the keyword counts were done after the main results were in, and are labelled that way here and in the study files. A separate AI reviewer went through the study before this was written, and its corrections are listed in the study's README.</p>
</aside>

## What to do with this

If something bugs you, it's probably the meter or the update, and you're not alone. Two things help there. Don't keep a year of work only inside one chat. Copy the parts that matter somewhere you own, because the app can change under you. And check what you're paying for against what the free tier and the other apps give you this month, because the limits move.

For the quiet risks, the trick is to make them loud on purpose. Ask it to argue against your idea before it agrees with it. When a fact matters, ask where it came from and open the source yourself. Look in the settings for whether your chats are used for training, and decide whether you mind. And for health, money or a big decision, treat it as the start of the homework, not the end.

Remember the five-star review that praised the opposite of over-reliance? It's from a Gemini user, and it puts this better than any of the papers:

<figure class="run">
<figcaption>Gemini, 5 stars, 9 November 2026</figcaption>
<blockquote><p>"It’s a helper, not a manager."</p></blockquote>

That's the same idea behind the last of the four questions on our [Get started](/start/#chat) page: what, other than you or the AI, would show the answer is right? It's the one question that turns a quiet risk into something you can check. <!-- Q7 -->
