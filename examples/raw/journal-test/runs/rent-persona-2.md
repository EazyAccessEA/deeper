Writer: claude-opus-5-5

## Prompt

Act as an expert financial planner. Should I rent or buy this flat?

=== MY SITUATION ===
- The flat: 2-bed in Leeds, asking price £280,000. Leasehold, 115 years left.
- Service charge plus ground rent: £1,800 a year.
- I have £46,500 saved. A 15% deposit is £42,000; legal fees, survey and other buying costs are about £4,500. I'm a first-time buyer.
- Mortgage offer: 85% loan, 4.6% fixed for 5 years, 25-year repayment term. Assume the rate stays 4.6% after the fix.
- Renting the same kind of flat nearby costs £1,250 a month. Rents here have gone up about 3% a year.
- If I rent, my savings sit in an account paying 4% a year, and I'd save any monthly difference there too.
- Upkeep on a flat like this is usually about 1% of the price a year.
- Selling costs (agent and legal) are about 1.5% of the sale price.
- I expect to stay about 5 years, maybe 7.
- I don't know what prices will do. The last few years round here were roughly +2% a year.

Nobody can answer questions before you reply. Do the task now with what's provided, and state any assumptions you make.

## Answer

# Rent vs Buy: 2-bed Leeds flat, £280,000

**Short answer:** Buying probably comes out ahead if you stay 5–7 years and prices grow at about the recent 2% a year. The advantage is modest, about £11k after 5 years and £22k after 7. The break-even is roughly 1–1.25% annual price growth, so the result depends heavily on a number nobody can predict. The bigger issue is that buying uses **every penny** of your £46,500, leaving no emergency fund. I'd fix that before buying.

---

## Assumptions

- **Stamp duty: £0.** First-time buyer relief in England covers purchases up to £300k (rules from April 2025). I've assumed your £4,500 buying costs cover everything else.
- **Mortgage:** £238,000 at 4.6% over 25 years, staying at 4.6% after the fix, as you asked.
- **Upkeep:** 1% of the flat's value each year, rising with the price (£2,800 in year 1).
- **Service charge and ground rent:** rising 3% a year. Leasehold charges rarely stay flat.
- **Rent:** £1,250 a month, rising 3% a year.
- **Renter's savings:** the £46,500 earns 4% a year, plus each month's cost difference is saved. I've treated the interest as tax-free (ISA plus Personal Savings Allowance), which is roughly right for a basic-rate taxpayer.
- **Selling costs:** 1.5% of the sale price. No early repayment charge, since you'd sell when or after the 5-year fix ends.
- **Excluded:** moving costs, furnishing, and insurance differences, which are small and roughly equal either way.

## What the numbers don't show

1. **No emergency fund.** £42,000 deposit plus £4,500 costs uses all £46,500. A boiler or appliance failure, a special service-charge levy, or a gap in income would go straight onto a credit card. This is my biggest concern.
2. **Flats are weaker investments than houses.** Across the UK, and in Leeds city-centre stock, flats have generally lagged houses in price growth. Assuming the area-wide 2% for a flat may be optimistic, and you're only about 1 point above break-even.
3. **Leasehold risks.** 115 years is fine; there's no lease-extension problem for decades. The real risks are rising service charges, major-works bills, and building-safety issues such as cladding. Ask the seller's managing agent for:
   - service-charge history and the budget
   - the reserve-fund balance
   - any planned major works
   - an EWS1 form if the building is over 11m
4. **Selling early is expensive.** Exit in years 1–3 and you'd likely lose money: the £4,500 buying costs, about £4,200 selling costs, plus an early repayment charge (often 3–5% of the balance, so £7k–£11k). Buying only makes sense if you're confident about the 5-year minimum.
5. **Rates after year 5.** If you remortgage at 6% instead of 4.6%, payments rise by about £170 a month. That would trim the 7-year advantage by roughly £3k–£4k. If savings rates fall below 4%, renting looks worse.
6. **Non-financial factors favour buying if you're settled:** security of tenure, freedom to decorate, and protection from rent rises. Renting gives you flexibility for job moves and relationship changes.

## My recommendation

**Buying is the better financial bet if you're confident you'll stay at least 5 years, but not at the cost of having zero cash reserves.** In order of preference:

1. **Negotiate the price down.** Every £10,000 off the price improves the buying outcome by about £12,000 after 5 years and frees up £1,500 of deposit. An offer around £265k–£270k would give you a margin of safety and some buffer cash.
2. **Or keep renting for 6–9 months** and save about £470 a month (the difference you'd otherwise pay) to build a £4k–£6k emergency fund before buying.
3. **Or compare a 10% deposit product.** This would free about £14,000 as a buffer. However, the rate will be higher, so get a broker to compare the total cost. Even 0.3–0.5% more interest could wipe out the advantage.

**Don't buy this flat if:** there's a real chance you'll move within 4 years, the service charge history shows big jumps or a thin reserve fund, or you can't build at least a small cash buffer.

*This is general modelling based on your figures, not regulated advice. A whole-of-market mortgage broker and your conveyancer should check the lease and the mortgage terms.*
